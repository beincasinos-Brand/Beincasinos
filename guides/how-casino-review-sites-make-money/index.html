How casino review sites make money

Understand common publisher business models and the signs of clear disclosure, evidence-based reviews, and editorial independence.

Educational guide · Updated 21 October 2026

Key point: earning money from an operator does not by itself prove a review is unreliable. What matters is transparent disclosure, evidence, and whether commercial relationships influence the editorial conclusion.

Affiliate commissions

An affiliate publisher may receive compensation when a reader clicks a tracked link or completes a contractually defined action. The agreement may use cost per acquisition (CPA), revenue share, or a hybrid model. Eligibility rules, calculation bases, exclusions, reporting and payment timing differ by contract.

These terms describe commercial arrangements; they are not a recommendation to register, deposit, or gamble. Commission incentives can create conflicts, so readers should check disclosures and the evidence behind a review.

Fixed-fee listings and sponsorship

A publisher may charge a fixed fee for a directory entry, display placement, or sponsored profile. Payment is not proof that an operator is licensed, suitable for a market, or independently recommended. Paid placements should be labelled clearly near the placement itself.

A fee must not buy a higher editorial score, a licence conclusion, a favourable complaint finding, or guaranteed inclusion in a “top” ranking. Editorial eligibility and market legality must remain separate from sales decisions.

How the main payment models differ

The labels describe how a publisher may be paid, not whether an operator is lawful or a review is trustworthy. The actual contract controls the calculation, eligible markets, reporting, and payment conditions.

ModelHow payment is generally calculatedWhat to check
Fixed listing feeA set amount for an agreed placement and term.Exact placement, dates, renewal, prominent label, cancellation and removal rules. A fee should not buy a positive review or a ranking.
CPAA fee for a contract-defined qualifying action.What counts as qualifying, attribution window, duplicate or invalid actions, reversals, eligible territories, reporting and payment timing.
Revenue shareA percentage of a contract-defined revenue base over a specified period.Precise calculation base, permitted deductions, negative balances if any, reporting, reconciliation, disputes, audit rights and termination treatment.
HybridA combination of fixed payment and performance-based commission.Document each component separately, identify overlapping incentives, and disclose the relationship clearly where required.

There is no universal rate or standard definition that applies to every affiliate programme. Two agreements using the same model name can produce different results. Never compare offers based on a headline rate alone.

Simple hypothetical examples

These examples are arithmetic only, not industry benchmarks, recommended prices, or predictions of income.

  • Fixed fee: an imaginary publisher charges $300 for a clearly labelled listing for one month. The amount does not prove the placement generated visits or sales.
  • CPA: an imaginary agreement pays $80 for each action that meets its written eligibility rules. Ten reported actions would imply $800 before any applicable reversals or adjustments; only the contract and reconciled records establish the amount actually due.
  • Revenue share: an imaginary agreement uses 20% of a defined $1,000 eligible revenue base, which gives $200 before any contract-specific adjustments. The phrase “20%” is meaningless without the calculation base and deductions.
  • Hybrid: an imaginary agreement combines a $100 fixed fee with an agreed performance component. The fixed fee and variable amount should be reported separately so the incentive is understandable.

Why a high commission is not proof of quality

Higher commercial compensation can create stronger incentives to feature one operator, but it does not establish better terms, safer products, legal market access, or better consumer outcomes. A credible review process keeps eligibility checks and editorial scoring separate from commercial negotiations, records conflicts, and is willing to reject or remove a paying partner.

Questions to ask when reading a review

  • Does the site explain whether it earns affiliate commission, listing fees, or sponsorship?
  • Is a paid placement labelled where it appears, not only in a policy page?
  • Does the methodology explain what evidence is checked and how scores are assigned?
  • Are the legal operator, exact domain, regulator record, and product scope identified for the relevant market?
  • Is there a meaningful “last checked” date and an explanation of what was not tested?
  • Are restrictions and negative findings described, or is every operator presented as a “best” choice?
  • Does the page distinguish verified facts from operator claims and untested reports?

Traffic and performance claims

Traffic, search rankings, clicks, conversions, and player-value estimates are different measurements. A publisher should not claim audience size or guaranteed results without dated evidence and a clear measurement method. Forecasts should be labelled as forecasts. A brand’s size or the existence of an affiliate programme does not prove willingness to buy a listing.

When the business model is unclear

Do not assume a review is independent merely because it looks detailed, or assume it is worthless merely because it earns revenue. Look for verifiable evidence, clear labels, market-specific scope, and a method that could lead to an unfavourable conclusion. If those are missing, treat the recommendation cautiously.
