Random outcomes: why past results do not predict the next one

Understand independent events, random number generators, and the limits of patterns in a sequence of results.

General educational information · Reviewed 21 October 2026

Key point: a streak of wins or losses does not, by itself, make a different result “due” next. Whether outcomes are independent depends on the rules and mechanics of the specific event. A sequence of past results is not enough to predict the next result of a properly independent random process.

What does an independent outcome mean?

Two events are independent when knowing the result of one does not change the probability of the other. For example, on a fair six-sided die, each face has a one-in-six chance on each roll if the rolls are independent and the die is fair. After several rolls show the same face, another face is not automatically more likely on the next roll.

This does not mean every real-world process is independent. In a finite shuffled deck, cards already drawn change which cards remain. Some games have stages, rules, or changing conditions that affect later probabilities. The correct model depends on the game’s mechanics—not on a pattern someone thinks they see.

What random number generators do

Digital games may use a random number generator (RNG) to produce values that are mapped to game outcomes under the game's rules. Technical standards may require outcomes to meet specified randomness, unpredictability, and fairness requirements. The details and legal scope depend on the regulator and product.

For Great Britain, the UK Gambling Commission’s Remote Gambling and Software Technical Standards include requirements concerning game rules and outcome generation. Its RTS 7 guidance on generation of random outcomes describes expectations for acceptably random outcomes and implementation in accordance with stated rules. These are Great Britain sources, not a universal statement of law.

Why “hot” and “cold” streaks can mislead

Random sequences can contain clusters, repeated outcomes, and long gaps. People naturally look for patterns, especially after a surprising run. But a pattern in a short sequence does not establish that the process has changed, nor does it show what the next outcome will be.

  • The “due” fallacy: assuming an outcome becomes more likely simply because it has not appeared recently.
  • The “hot streak” assumption: assuming recent results guarantee that the same outcome will continue.
  • Selective memory: remembering apparent patterns that fit a belief and overlooking the sequences that do not.
  • Confusing averages with next results: a theoretical long-run percentage does not provide a schedule for individual outcomes.

How this differs from RTP and house edge

Return to player (RTP) is a theoretical long-run return measure for a specified game and ruleset. House edge describes the corresponding theoretical advantage under stated assumptions. Neither number tells you which result will occur next, and neither is a guarantee of an individual session’s outcome.

What can a past-results record establish?

A record can describe what happened in that sample. On its own, it usually cannot prove that a game is fair or unfair, reveal the next result, or verify a particular operator’s compliance. A meaningful technical assessment needs the correct game version, rules, data, statistical method, and appropriate expertise. Claims about a specific game or operator should be checked against relevant primary documentation and regulator information.

Questions worth asking

  • Are the rules and probabilities clearly documented for this exact game version?
  • Is the information from the regulator, a technical testing report, or another identifiable primary source?
  • Does the claim distinguish an observed sample from a theoretical probability?
  • Is someone using a supposed pattern to sell a prediction, system, or guaranteed outcome?

Be cautious of anyone promising a reliable way to predict random results from recent outcomes. No pattern analysis can turn an independent random process into a guaranteed prediction.
